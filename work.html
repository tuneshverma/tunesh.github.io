01 / Webtiga · Oct 2023 — Nov 2024

A platform for turning a company's own data into its own model.

Create Brain took a customer's documents, calls and recordings and produced a fine-tuned model they could deploy. The harder lesson was everything that had to be built around that model before it behaved.

Create Brain · self-service fine-tuning platform

From raw customer data to a deployed model

SageMaker · Airflow · Mistral · Llama 3

Organisations have domain knowledge sitting in PDFs, call recordings and video, and no route from that to a model of their own. Create Brain was the route: a drag-and-drop pipeline where a customer configured the stages and the platform handled ingestion, dataset creation, training and deployment without anyone writing the ML workflow by hand.

Fine-tuning was the easy half. The models we could actually use — Mistral and Meta Llama 3 — were small open-weight models, and they were not reliable out of the box.

What went wrong

  • Hallucination — answers that weren't grounded in the customer's material, which is the one failure a domain model cannot have.
  • Weak instruction following — conversational constraints were applied inconsistently.
  • Thin conversational memory — little useful context held across a longer conversation.

What we built around it

  • Substantial prompt, instruction and guardrail engineering per application.
  • Memory handled outside the model: a call before the LLM call retrieved and prepared the relevant history.
  • Context assembly, conversational control and orchestration at the application layer rather than trusting the model's own context handling.
MaiSupport · client POC

Recovery stories, turned into a system that can use them

Whisper-style STT · PII removal · Mistral · Qdrant · RAG

A client working in gambling-addiction recovery ran a structured programme: defined steps, specific conversations that had to happen at each one, and progress recorded in their CRM. They also had thousands of recordings of people describing how they had come through addiction themselves. The task was a conversational system that could hold a genuinely domain-specific conversation while moving someone through that programme.

The recordings were the asset and the problem at once. They are real people talking about the worst period of their lives, so nothing could be trained on until the identities were gone.

At conversation time the system draws on both of those, which is the part worth being precise about — it was never expected to rely on what the model had memorised.

All of that ran inside the client's programme rather than as an open chat, so the conversation had to know which step the person was on and steer toward it.

What I worked on

  • Transcribing thousands of session recordings, and the cleaning pass over the raw transcripts.
  • Generating the question-and-answer data from the processed transcripts.
  • Fine-tuning Mistral on the prepared dataset.
  • Qdrant ingestion and the retrieval side of the RAG pipeline.
  • Prompt engineering for conversational behaviour and instruction adherence.
  • Testing the system and working on hallucination and instruction-following failures.

What the system also involved

The identity-removal pipeline and the CRM integration were part of the wider build rather than my own work. They are described here because the project does not make sense without them — thousands of recordings of real people cannot be used at all without the first, and the programme has nowhere to record progress without the second.

TATA AIA · supporting project

Insurance retrieval

RAG

A retrieval-based conversational system over client-provided insurance material, pulling the relevant information from the domain knowledge base and passing it to the model as context at answer time.

My involvement here was limited next to Create Brain and MaiSupport, so this stays short rather than being written up as a major personal project.
Optimis · supporting project

Medical codes from a consultation

Speech to text · RAG

A healthcare application for doctors. The audio of a patient consultation was transcribed, and that conversation became the input to a retrieval pipeline which fetched the relevant medical code from the domain knowledge base — so the coding work that normally follows a consultation had a starting point.

I worked around the AI side — the audio-to-text and retrieval pipeline — rather than the healthcare product as a whole, so this is kept brief for the same reason.
Create Agent · the orchestration layer

Where my work ended

RAG · Tools · Conditional routing · Loops

Create Agent was the other half of the platform: a visual system for assembling AI applications by dragging, connecting and configuring components — RAG for domain knowledge, conditional routing to direct execution on a condition or a model's output, tools for reaching external services, loops for repeated execution, and LLM components for the reasoning itself. Underneath the canvas it was considerably more complex than it looked, because something had to manage execution across all those connected pieces.

I was not the primary architect of Create Agent and don't claim to have been. It is in this write-up because the platform only makes sense as two halves, and knowing where one person's work stops is part of describing a platform honestly.
02 / Vision Group · Jul 2020 — Sep 2023

Retail perception, from the cooler to the shelf.

Three years of production computer vision: a vending cooler that charges with no checkout, shelf intelligence that counts stock it cannot fully see, and two research efforts that pointed at what came next.

Autonomous Retail · Smart Vending Cooler

From detection to a transaction you can trust

YOLO · Classification · Centroid tracking · DeepStream · Jetson

A customer scanned a barcode to unlock the cooler, opened the door, took one or more beverages, and closed it. The amount was deducted from their wallet automatically. The hard part was never spotting a bottle. It was working out what the customer picked up, whether it actually left the cooler, whether it came back, and which SKU was ultimately taken — because getting that wrong means charging someone for a drink they put back.

Training the detector

  • Captured a substantial dataset across camera viewpoints and operating conditions.
  • Covered angles from the top to the bottom of the cooler, varied lighting, and camera fogging.
  • Included partial obstruction, different ways customers hold bottles, and different product positions.
  • Videos were annotated by hand, then heavily augmented for robustness.
  • Reached 98%+ detection accuracy during internal testing.

Why two models, not one

A single detector trained on every client's SKUs would need retraining each time a catalogue changed, and would grow harder to train as products multiplied. Splitting the problem meant a new client only required a new classifier over the same detector.

Picked, returned, picked again

Detection and classification still aren't enough. A customer can lift a bottle, change their mind, put it back, and take a different one. A system that charges for whatever leaves the frame would bill them for both.

The hard part: one symptom, four suspects

The real engineering difficulty was raising end-to-end reliability, because every failure looks identical from the outside. A customer is charged the wrong amount — and nothing in that outcome tells you which stage broke.

Two deployments, one pipeline

The same DeepStream pipeline shipped in two configurations, and what differs between them is simply where the video is processed — and therefore what has to cross the network.

What this project actually was

Not "I trained a YOLO model". Detection, classification, tracking, temporal reasoning, edge deployment and remote model management, composed into a system that takes money from someone's wallet without a human in the loop. The interesting engineering lives in the seams between those parts, not inside any one of them.

What I took from it

That a pipeline's accuracy is not the product of its stages' accuracies — it is limited by whichever stage you cannot observe. Making failure attributable per component did more for reliability than any single model improvement.

The 98%+ figure is detection accuracy measured during internal testing, not end-to-end transaction accuracy. I don't have a separate measured number for the complete system, so I'm not claiming one — and latency, throughput and deployment counts are omitted for the same reason.
Planogram · Retail Shelf Intelligence

Counting stock the camera cannot see

U-Net · DPT · Stitching · Detection · Classification

Store managers needed shelf state without a stocktake, so they photographed shelves on their phones and the system worked out what was there and how much of it. A full shelf rarely fits in one frame, so images were stitched before anything downstream could run. The counting itself is where it gets interesting — a photograph is a poor witness to inventory, and it is wrong in two opposite directions at once.

So the pipeline corrects the raw detection count twice, in opposite directions, using two different models.

Why segmentation, not a better detector

A product on the shelf behind is a perfectly valid detection — the detector is not wrong, it is answering a different question. No amount of detector accuracy fixes it, because the error is about which shelf a correct detection belongs to. That is a segmentation problem, so U-Net isolated the target shelf region before detection ran.

Why depth mattered

Shelf stock is arranged front to back, so the visible face of a row says nothing about how deep it goes. Depth estimation with DPT was explored to reason about products positioned behind one another, turning a count of visible items into something closer to a count of items present.

Proof of concept · not deployed

Early LLMs for retail

Llama 2 · Local inference

When the first open-weight LLMs arrived, we wanted to know whether they were useful to a retail business yet. I ran Llama 2 locally, fed it relevant retail material, and explored what a retail-facing chatbot could and could not answer.

This was a feasibility study, not a product. It never went to production, and the value was in finding out where the models fell short — which is also where my later RAG and grounding work started.
Proof of concept · research

2D photographs to a 3D product

Stitching · Alignment · Reconstruction

A research effort to build a 3D representation of a product from ordinary photographs taken around it. The work was in stitching and aligning views captured from different sides, then reconstructing geometry from them.

Also exploratory. No production deployment and no measured reconstruction quality to report, so none is claimed.
03 / Freelance · Feb — Jun 2020

Two contracts, two very different cameras.

Short engagements between roles: one counting human movement in real time, the other measuring manufactured parts. Both proofs of concept, and both interesting for the architecture rather than the model.

Proof of concept · real-time pose

Counting push-ups, and judging them

MediaPipe Pose · Joint angles · Parallel regions

A camera watches someone doing push-ups and counts the reps. MediaPipe Pose gave skeleton landmarks for head, shoulders, arms, torso and legs; the angles between those joints are what actually decide anything. A rep is a transition through the expected posture, so the same angles that count a good push-up also identify a bad one — the system separated correct repetitions from incorrect ones rather than counting every movement.

The part worth showing is how two people were handled on one camera. Rather than running a single pose pipeline across the whole frame, the frame was divided into equal regions and each region processed independently.

Why angles, not appearance

A push-up is defined by geometry, not by what a person looks like. Deriving elbow, shoulder and torso angles from landmarks makes the judgement independent of body type, clothing and camera distance, and gives a continuous signal to detect the transitions that mark a repetition.

Why split the frame

One pose pipeline over a frame with two people has to solve association — which limb belongs to whom — before it can count anything. Cropping to a region removes that problem instead of solving it, and each pipeline then faces the single-person case it is good at.

A proof of concept. No deployment, and no measured counting accuracy to report, so none is claimed.
Proof of concept · industrial inspection

Measuring brake pads for defects

OpenCV · Contours · Template matching · Custom CNN

A contract for a manufacturer supplying brake pads to the electronics and electrical industry. The task was inspecting manufactured pads for defects and dimensional problems: overall pad geometry, how many holes there were, the diameter of each, and whether they sat in the right positions.

Why not reach for a model first

A hole diameter is a measurement, not a prediction. Classical techniques return it exactly, are inspectable when they go wrong, and need no training data — all of which matter on a factory line. Reaching for a learned model here would have traded a correct answer for an approximate one.

What the mix bought

Splitting by question type meant each requirement met the technique suited to it, rather than forcing one approach across the whole inspection. The geometric checks stayed deterministic while the harder visual patterns could be explored separately.

A proof of concept. I don't have a measured defect-detection accuracy from this project, so no figure is quoted.
04 / MintM · Mar 2019 — Jan 2020

Retail intelligence from CCTV.

Two different systems: one converted existing CCTV streams into customer-behavior analytics; the other connected computer vision to a physical conveyor system for automated return processing.

Retail Footfall & Customer Behavior Analytics

Turning CCTV into structured retail data

YOLO · Tracking · ResNet · Heatmaps

The business objective was to transform existing CCTV infrastructure in retail stores into useful data for stakeholders: how many people enter and leave, which doors they use, visitor gender distribution, and where customers spend time inside the store.

Footfall & tracking

  • Used existing CCTV cameras and processed multiple camera streams.
  • YOLO detected people and centroid tracking maintained movement over time.
  • Virtual lines were placed around entry/exit regions to calculate IN/OUT counts.
  • A single camera could support multiple doors or entry/exit regions.
  • Tracking logic was strengthened to make counting more reliable.

Gender classification

  • Person bounding-box crops from the detector were collected and manually labeled.
  • Created roughly 5,000 male + 5,000 female samples.
  • Trained a ResNet-based classifier and integrated it with the detection pipeline.
  • Every detected person could be classified in parallel as male/female.
  • About 91% accuracy was observed on the deployed system during team testing.

Heatmaps & dwell behavior

Because each detected person had a tracked centroid, the same data could be used to measure spatial concentration and how long tracked people remained in particular areas. This enabled store heatmaps and customer-density analysis.

Why it mattered

The system moved beyond simple people counting. It created a data layer over existing video infrastructure so retail stakeholders could study traffic, demographics and customer movement patterns.

BigBasket · Return-to-Vendor

Automated barcode verification on a conveyor

Computer Vision · Image Processing · State Machine · Tracking

A real-time warehouse system for identifying returned products. Returned items were placed onto a conveyor, brought in front of a camera, scanned, and verified against the barcode/product dataset supplied by BigBasket.

Reliable barcode reading

  • Used barcode-reading libraries together with image-processing techniques.
  • Tracked the product through a defined virtual rectangular region.
  • The system continuously evaluated whether the barcode had been read successfully.

Closed-loop state machine

  • If the barcode was read, the item could continue forward after verification.
  • If reading failed, the conveyor could reverse and present the item to the camera again.
  • If it still failed, the conveyor could stop for a defined period to allow another read.
  • The computer-vision result directly controlled physical conveyor behavior.

Deployment

The system was deployed in a BigBasket warehouse. You visited the deployment environment to monitor accuracy and speed and validate behavior on real returned products.

Testing

Testing covered roughly 500 items. Barcode reading was reported at above 90% accuracy, with successful reads then verified against the client-provided dataset.

This project is an important early example of building a complete AI-enabled physical system—not just a model: perception → decision → state machine → machine control.
05 / Synergy Labs · Jun 2018 — Dec 2018

Two separate computer-vision POCs.

These projects were distinct and should remain separate in the portfolio: one focused on facial recognition and the other on document table understanding.

Facial Recognition

One-shot learning for office attendance

Triplet Loss · Face Recognition · Realtime Camera

The POC explored state-of-the-art facial-recognition approaches available at the time, including a triplet-loss approach associated with one-shot / single-shot learning.

Learning setup

  • Training used an anchor image, a positive image of the same person, and a negative image from a different person.
  • The objective was to learn a representation that separated same-person and different-person faces.
  • Approximately five images per employee were collected for enrollment.

Attendance workflow

A camera was placed in the office. Faces were recognized from the live stream and the recognition result was used to record attendance. This was a POC rather than a production-scale deployment.

Document Intelligence

Table detection & structured extraction

YOLO · Image Processing · Line Detection · Excel

Normal document extraction can work well for plain text, but complex tables require understanding the visual structure of rows, columns and cells. This POC focused on detecting tables and converting their contents into structured spreadsheet data.

Dataset & detection

  • Created and annotated roughly 3,000 table data points.
  • Used object detection techniques, including YOLO, to locate tables and relevant regions.
  • Built processing paths for tables with visible lines and tables without clear lines.

Extraction pipeline

  • Used image processing and line detection to reason about rows and columns.
  • Determined coordinates for relevant table regions/cells.
  • Extracted values from those coordinates and converted them into structured data.
  • Exported the resulting information into Excel sheets.
Built in 2018, this was an early document-AI problem tackled with computer vision, annotation, object detection and custom image-processing logic rather than today's much more readily available document/LLM tooling.
06 / Early AI experiments

Learning by building.

Before professional AI work, experimentation with image classification helped establish the foundations for model architecture, evaluation and generalization.

Blood Cell Classifier

Learning CNNs through image classification

Keras · CNN · InceptionV3 · Xception

Built an image-classification pipeline using the Kaggle Blood Cell Images dataset, with four classes: Eosinophil, Lymphocyte, Monocyte and Neutrophil.

What I explored

  • Built a CNN architecture from scratch.
  • Experimented with established architectures including InceptionV3 and Xception.
  • Used image augmentation and evaluated accuracy, loss and F1-style performance measures.

Learning objective

The goal was not only to maximize accuracy, but to understand how architecture choices, training behavior and generalization affect real model performance.
